Security is becoming signed lifecycle evidence.

A lower-volume holiday week still produced a clear direction: prioritize actively exploited weaknesses, bind agent authority to verifiable records, require evidence before restoration, and plan identity and cryptography across their full lifecycle.

CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities: Fifteen Additions in Three Days

Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency · 23–25 November 2026 · KEV Catalog

What is genuinely new: CISA added two exploited vulnerabilities on 23 November, three on 24 November, and ten on 25 November. The concentrated update is operational evidence that vulnerability prioritization must follow observed exploitation, not severity scores alone.

Why it matters: Public agencies have finite patch windows and complex vendor dependencies. A rapid KEV expansion can overwhelm teams that lack authoritative asset ownership, exposure data, compensating controls, and escalation paths.

Practical implication: Automate KEV-to-asset matching; require accountable owners, due dates, exception evidence, external-exposure checks, and verification that remediation actually removed the vulnerable condition.

AI Audit Reference Architecture for Post-Hoc Agent Accountability

IETF individual Internet-Draft, revision 02 · 24 November 2026 · Draft

What is genuinely new: The draft proposes signed, purpose-limited records that allow principals and affected parties to verify what autonomous or semi-autonomous agents did in a principal’s name.

Why it matters: Conventional logs often show API calls but not delegated authority, purpose, evidence custody, or the parties to a disputed action.

Practical implication: Require agent records to bind human or organizational principal, agent identity, delegated authority, action, evidence source, time, result, and permitted secondary use. This is an individual draft—not an endorsed IETF standard.

Restoration Evidence Records for AI Agent Incident Response

IETF individual Internet-Draft, revision 01 · 24 November 2026 · Draft

What is genuinely new: The draft defines machine-checkable evidence supporting a decision to restore an AI agent after containment, including role attribution, remediation proof, replay testing, authorization, and residual-risk documentation.

Why it matters: Stopping an agent is straightforward; deciding when it can safely regain credentials, tools, and network access is a harder governance problem.

Practical implication: Add a formal restoration gate to AI incident playbooks. Re-enable authority only after signed evidence shows remediation, recurrence testing, accountable approval, scoped access, and accepted residual risk. Treat this draft as work in progress.

Additional Priority Reading

Common Access Card Best Practices for Government Affiliates

National Security Agency · 23 November 2026 · NSA guidance index

What is genuinely new: NSA published updated custody and handling guidance for CACs and notes that the principles also apply to PIV cards, FIDO authenticators, and other tokens.

Why it matters: Phishing-resistant authentication still depends on lifecycle discipline: personal custody, rapid loss reporting, revocation, issuance controls, and protection against misuse at shared endpoints.

Practical implication: Align PIV/FIDO policy, help-desk procedures, physical-access processes, certificate revocation, and privileged-workstation controls as one credential lifecycle.

Semantic Governance Requirements for AI Agent Delegation and Handoff

IETF individual Internet-Draft, revision 03 · 21 November 2026 · Draft

What is genuinely new: The revision explores explicit semantics for agent-to-agent delegation, including authority scope, constraints, provenance, revocation, and handoff accountability.

Why it matters: Multi-agent systems can silently amplify authority when one agent passes work, credentials, or context to another.

Practical implication: Prohibit implicit authority inheritance. Every handoff should carry bounded purpose, permitted tools and data, expiry, provenance, accountable owner, and revocation state. This is not an IETF standard.

Post-Quantum Lifecycle Profiles for OPC UA, DNP3 Secure Authentication, and WireGuard

IETF individual Internet-Drafts · 24 November 2026 · OPC UA · DNP3 · WireGuard

What is genuinely new: Three coordinated revisions apply post-quantum authentication and key lifecycle concepts to protocols used in industrial, IoT, and secure-network deployments.

Why it matters: Critical infrastructure cannot treat PQC as a data-center-only migration. Long-lived field assets, vendor gateways, constrained devices, and operational maintenance cycles create distinct constraints.

Practical implication: Add protocol-specific crypto inventories, firmware feasibility, key rollover, interoperability, outage, rollback, and vendor-support evidence to OT modernization plans. These are individual drafts, not approved profiles.

Executive Takeaway

  • Exploit intelligence must drive remediation. KEV-to-asset matching and verified closure should outrank generic severity queues.
  • Agent accountability must survive delegation and incidents. Record authority, handoffs, actions, containment, restoration evidence, and residual risk.
  • Identity and cryptography are lifecycle systems. Token custody and post-quantum migration both require issuance, rotation, revocation, recovery, and auditability.

Emerging pattern: Security architecture is moving from static control statements to signed lifecycle evidence—who held authority, what changed, why access was restored, and whether the result can be independently verified.
